User-facing offerwall and survey pages disclose internal provider setup metadata
Low
Operator Metadata Disclosure
CashClip's browser-facing offerwall and survey screens reveal internal business setup details to anyone who can load those pages. A visitor can learn which ad and survey partners the company uses, the exact environment variable names expected for their secrets, and the partner dashboard URLs, which makes targeted phishing and social-engineering attacks against operators easier.

The OfferwallsScreen and SurveysScreen components render hardcoded setup guidance whenever provider configuration data is absent. In artifacts/cashclip/app/offerwalls.tsx:168-171, the page queries /api/offerwalls/providers, and in artifacts/cashclip/app/surveys.tsx:138-142, it queries /api/surveys/providers. Those API routes are admin-gated on the backend, so non-admin and unauthenticated visitors will not receive provider state. However, the frontend treats a missing/failed response as anyConfigured === false and falls back to rendering operator setup instructions.

That fallback content includes sensitive operational metadata on normal user-facing pages. In artifacts/cashclip/app/offerwalls.tsx:305-321, the page exposes the monetization partner list (Tapjoy, AdGate Media, OfferToro, Lootably, Adjoe), the exact secret names expected by operations (TAPJOY_SDK_KEY, ADGATE_API_KEY, OFFERTORO_API_KEY, LOOTABLY_API_KEY, ADJOE_SDK_KEY + ADJOE_APP_ID), and the corresponding provider dashboard domains. In artifacts/cashclip/app/surveys.tsx:279-300, it similarly discloses survey partners and secret names (POLLFISH_API_KEY, CINT_API_KEY, LUCID_API_KEY).

An attacker does not need administrative access to exploit this. They can simply browse to the web app's /offerwalls or /surveys routes, or inspect the shipped frontend bundle, and collect the company's monetization stack and secret naming conventions. Under this threat model, operator metadata embedded into public/browser-delivered code is security-relevant because it materially lowers the cost of convincing staff to divulge the right credentials or targeting the right third-party accounts.

This is a low-severity information disclosure rather than a direct account takeover, because no secret values are exposed. The root cause is that admin-oriented setup content is embedded in production user-facing screens and shown as an error fallback instead of being withheld entirely from non-admin contexts.
